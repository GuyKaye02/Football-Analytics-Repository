Who should Bellinghampass to?

xPV: a decision-making framework for passing

A midfielder plays a forward pass. It finds a team-mate, and the standard metrics reward him for it. But was it the right ball? xPV, expected passing value, grades the decision rather than the outcome. At the instant a pass is released it values every team-mate as a possible target, then asks whether the player picked the best one, whether or not the pass came off.

By Guy Kaye. Soccermatics Project 3, built on 98 Real Madrid matches of SkillCorner tracking data, 2023/24 and 2024/25.

    25:33Real Madrid v Granada, LaLiga

    The ball the model agrees with

    Kroos has the ball outside the box. Brahim Díaz is starting a run in behind. Kroos doesn't play it to where Brahim is; he plays it to where Brahim is going.

    This is where a naive pass model breaks. It would only evaluate the option based on where Brahim currently is, at his feet, which misses what the option really is: not a ball to where he is standing, but the ball in behind, into the space he is running onto. Judged at his feet, one of the best balls on the pitch looks like an ordinary one, and Kroos gets marked down for playing it.

    xPV therefore values the pass two ways: to the player's feet, and into his run. Each option keeps whichever is worth more.

    Priced where Brahim is going, the ball is both easier to complete and far more dangerous. The model rates it the best option on the pitch, and it's the one Kroos chose.

    The model finds that meeting point from the runner's speed and direction and a realistic ball speed, then re-checks everything at the new spot: the passing lane, the interception risk, whether Kroos can strike it in that direction, and what the position is worth.

    Because the decision is priced at the moment of release, Kroos would be credited for choosing this ball even if it had been cut out. The choice was right; whether it came off is a separate question.

    For an analyst, this is the difference between a metric that punishes through-balls and one that can tell you which midfielders see them.

    An open lane isn't always an available pass

    Forward passes are worth more, so a model that only asks whether the lane is open and the target is valuable will nearly always tell a player to go forward. An open lane isn't enough. Just as important is which way the passer is facing, and whether he has time to turn.

    Early against Athletic, Bellingham receives with his momentum carrying him back towards his own goal at 4 m/s, a defender 4.4 m away and closing. On a pitch-control map, a forward ball to Vinícius looks on. On the pitch, it isn't: Bellingham is facing the wrong way.

    The executability gate, g, measures exactly this. For every option it runs two clocks: how long the passer needs to turn and strike, and how long before pressure arrives. If the turn takes longer than the time available, the option is suppressed, however open the lane.

    Without g, the model's best option would almost always be a forward ball, including ones the passer could never have played, and nearly every decision would look like a mistake. With it, a player is only marked against options he could actually have taken.

    That changes the verdict here. The ball to Alaba goes backwards, so xT records it as a small negative: by that measure Bellingham has just made his team slightly worse off. xPV compares it against what was actually available to a player facing his own goal with a defender arriving in under a second, and he isn't penalised for the sensible pass.

    What an analyst gets from it

    Because every option at every pass has a price, each decision can be scored several ways. Summed over a season, they give a profile of how a player chooses, not just what he produces.

    The coach's questionThe numberWhat it measures

    Was the best ball on, and did he find it?Chose-best %How often he played the highest-valued option.

    What did he leave on the table?RegretThe best option's value minus the one he played.

    Does it agree with what we already know?

    Across Real Madrid's 25 regular passers, xPV value added tracks the established threat metrics closely, with correlations of about 0.45 to 0.76 against the xT family. Players who beat the typical choice also tend to create threat by conventional measures.

    Chose-best and decision percentile behave differently. They show little relationship with xT or Shaw's EPV. Threat metrics are driven largely by where a player receives and moves the ball; these two ask only how good his choice was among what was on. That is the part existing numbers can't see. Whether it holds as a stable skill over larger samples is the next test.

    In practice

    Video review. Rank a match by value added and the decisions worth clipping rise to the top, including completed passes that look fine on a stats sheet.

    Opposition scouting. See which options a playmaker reliably takes from which positions. A predictable passer is easier to press into his favourite ball.

    Recruitment. Separate how well a player chooses from how much his role lets him produce, so a midfielder in a side with less of the ball is judged on the options he actually had.

    Under the hood

    For every team-mate i at the release frame, the option is worth its upside if it arrives plus its downside if it's lost:

    xPVi = Pcmp,i · Vcmp,i + (1 − Pcmp,i) · Vturnover,i with Pcmp = Pmodel × g. The decision is then scored against the selection-weighted value of the situation, Σ πi · xPVi, which is what an average choice from that exact position is worth. Players making a run are priced twice, at their feet and at the projected meeting point, keeping the better of the two.

    * The case figures above label these V+ and V−: V+ is Vcmp, the value if the ball arrives, and V− is Vturnover, the cost if it is lost.

    What it can't see yet

    Retention is undervalued. Position values are learned from shots later in the possession, and only about one possession in ten ends in one. A pass that keeps the ball, resets the attack or draws pressure earns little credit.

    Body shape is inferred. Tracking data has no pose, so facing direction comes from movement or the incoming pass. When that is uncertain the gate backs off rather than guess.

    Space is a single point. The meeting point assumes the runner holds his speed and line. A jog that becomes a sprint gets under-projected, because at the moment of the pass the two look the same.

    It grades who, not whether. xPV scores the choice of receiver once a pass is made. It doesn't yet ask whether he should have shot, carried, or passed a second earlier.
